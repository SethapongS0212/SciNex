Section: Strabismus classification

Text:
Machine learning models for strabismus classification The final analytical stage involved classifying strabismus based on landmark-derived features, as illustrated in Fig. We evaluated and compared seven supervised machine learning classifiers11. • Artificial Neural Network (ANN). • Support Vector Machine (SVM). The classification was performed using 30 features per participant, derived from five geometric ratios (RT1– RT5) calculated across six ocular observations (two eyes per image across three gaze positions). Training process To ensure rigorous performance assessment, the following experimental protocol was implemented: 1) The dataset was labeled binary, where 'True' represented the presence of strabismus and 'False' indicated orthotropia. 2) Our experiment conducted 4-fold cross-validation by splitting data into 4 partitions. Each partition con tained 25 strabismus cases and 12–13 orthotropia subjects. 3) In each fold, the training set was oversampled using SMOTE with five nearest neighbors and a sampling strategy designed to have 200 samples for each class. Then, all machine learning models were fitted to the oversampled training set, and the performances of test set prediction were evaluated. 4) At the final step, the performance metrics: accuracy , sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV or precision), and negative predictive value (NPV), were averaged and reported.

While this latency is acceptable for a prototype, further optimization—such as utilizing GPU acceleration or migrating to on-device inference—could enhance scalability and reduce dependency on network connectivity in high-traffic or resource-limited settings.

Section: Interpretation of results

Text:
By employing RT-DETR for landmark localization and biometric ratio–based classification via traditional ML models, we demonstrated promising screening performance under controlled image acquisition conditions. Across the RT-DETR models trained for each gaze, landmark detection reached acceptable reliability with IoU = 0.62 and mean center-point error of 6.52 pixels, despite the absence of clinical-grade imaging. These results match or exceed prior ocular landmark detection systems. For instance, Simoes et al.17 used U-Net and ResNet to detect ocular alignment in digital images , achieving strong detection performance but limited generalizability beyond controlled settings. Our model expanded on this by using RT-DETR, which enabled real-time inference and robust performance across three gaze directions. The use of biometric landmark ratios provided a more interpretable representation for strabismus classification than an end-to-end image classifier. Similar to earlier work by Mengash et al.22, who applied rule- based and CNN methods to video analysis for detecting horizontal strabismus22, our method demonstrated comparable screening performance— accuracy of 0.95, sensitivity of 0.96, and specificity of 0.94—using only still images from smartphones. In contrast, Seo et al.9 reported 0.93 accuracy with an infrared video –based system that required specialized hardware.

Section: Interpretation of results

Text:
Our proof-of-concept framework used smartphone-acquired images collected under controlled conditions and required three static photographs for binary screening of strabismus versus orthotropia. Additionally, Valente et al.8 employed a cover-test–based automated diagnosis with digital video , reporting accuracy of 0.94, sensitivity of 0.97, and specificity = 0.73. While they achieved slightly higher sensitivity, our approach showed higher specificity. High specificity is clinically advantageous in screening contexts to minimize unnecessary referrals and parental anxiety resulting from false positives. Furthermore, their system relied on motion cues, whereas ours relied on still images and landmark geometry, thus reducing environmental constraints and allowing asynchronous screening . From an architectural perspective, prior mobile-based AI attempts typically used CNNs without landmark explanation6. In contrast, our framework uses human-readable input features (RT1–RT5) derived from ocular geometry, which may facilitate clinical inspection of model inputs and improve transparency relative to end- to-end image classifiers. Nevertheless, feature importance and rule extraction from a Random Forest should be interpreted as providing partial interpretability of how the model separates classes within this dataset, rather than a clinically exhaustive or directly causal explanation of ocular misalignment.
