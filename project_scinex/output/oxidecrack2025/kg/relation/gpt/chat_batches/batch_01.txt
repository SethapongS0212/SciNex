Text:
Material degradation is one of the main problems in various high- temperature processes, directly resulting in the failure of the material. Crack and protective oxide film spallation caused either by mechanical stress development in the oxidation process or thermal stress due to a mismatch of the thermal expansions of the formed oxide and alloy are common forms of failure in high- temperature processes. Typically, the Pilling-Bedworth ratio (PBR) is employed to predict crack and spallation of the oxide by determining the volume changes of oxide and alloy because of its simplicity. However, this approach provides poor crack and spallation predictions. Hence, machine learning was adopted in the present work to predict oxide formation and spallation in the temperature range of 600-1,200 °C. The inputs for the present developed model were alloy compositions, oxide formed during oxidation , and oxidation conditions and periods. Furthermore, the predicted results of the present developed machine learning model were compared to those obtained by the PBR method. The present results revealed that the accuracy of the oxide spallation prediction of the present model was better than that of the PBR method. The random forest with 15 estimators was the best machine learning model. Finally, it can be concluded that the machine learning model is essential for accurate material failure prediction.

Section: Abstract

Text:
In high temperature processes, the alloys usually deploy as structural and process equipment due to their high melting point, proper mechanical properties and high oxidation resistance1,2. The resistance to high temperature deterioration is a result of the protective oxide scales formed on the alloy surfaces which protect the alloy from environmental attack3–6. Hence, whenever the scales well adhere on the alloy surface , the deteriorations of the alloys are supposed to be not occurred. On the contrary, if the crack and/or spallation, which are the signs of non-protective, of the scale occurs, the chemical reactions promoting the failures of the materials would be accelerated leading to catastrophic of the materials. Crack and spallation could occur by the stress development in the oxide scale reaching the critical strength of oxide . The stress in the oxide scale can be distinguished into 2 types7: the thermal stress due to the thermal expansion mismatch of oxide formed and the substrate and the growth stress developing during the oxidation process5,8,9. The crack due to thermal stress can be easily estimated by considering the thermal expansion coefficients (CTE) at high temperature of the substrate and the oxides formed10,11. If the difference in both CTEs is large, the use of such materials should be avoided.

For the growth stress, it is more complicated and believed that the stress developed is a result of many factors12, i.e., the volumes and crystal structures of substrate and oxide and the oxide growth mechanism. The equation evaluating the volume change due to the oxide formation during oxidation was established in 1923 by Pilling and Bedworth and it is known as Pilling-Bedworth ratio abbreviated as PBR Eq. By taking the comparison of the volumes of oxide formed and the metal used, it helps ascribe the protectiveness of the oxide . PBR gives the protection performance of the oxide scale . PBR < 1 means the oxide formed does not fully cover the metal surface due to the tensile stress, so the oxide is not protective. The best performance occurs when PBR is close to 1 or in the range of 1 to 2. The larger PBR implies high compressive stress in the oxide film, thus the crack tends to occur13–16. Nevertheless, PBR was established based on several assumptions which are sometimes not consistent with the real situation leading to the poor predictions14. Some oxides externally grow at the oxide -gas interface opposing to the assumption of PBR that the oxides grow at the metal- oxide interface3,18–22. PBR does not consider the substrate and component geometries which affect the stress distribution in the oxides. Also, the self-healing and volatilisations of oxide components during the oxidation is not taken into account by PBR14.
